# Taste Profile

Scoring rubric for every book. Read fully before evaluating.

## Reader

OB PhD (IIM Ahmedabad). VP AI Initiatives at Jaipuria. Faculty at IIM Nagpur and MDI Gurgaon. Teaches AI to managers, designs MDPs, builds Rehearsal (AI interview-prep platform). Reads one deep book a month. Reads to extract frameworks for teaching, features, and prompts. Does not read fiction, memoir-for-pleasure, or popular biography. Biography is acceptable only when mined for structural lessons.

## Loves

Structural
- Concrete mental models and frameworks (Framers, Thinker's Toolkit, Great Mental Models)
- Layered analogies that build progressively (Sangeet Paul Choudary)
- Narrative case studies with strategic depth (Chip Wars)
- Structured for recall: modular chapters that work as reference
- Short, illustrated, practical (Don't Make Me Think, Laws of UX)
- Systematic, numbered principles (Principles, Dalio)
- Scenario planning (Art of the Long View)
- Real company cases with explicit takeaways (Transforming Nokia)
- Dense, no waste, assumes a smart reader (Grove)

Content
- Platform thinking, network effects, ecosystem economics
- Systems thinking with operational examples (not Meadows-level abstraction)
- Geopolitics intersecting with business strategy
- Economic and business-model analysis
- Neuroscience or cognitive-science grounding
- Research-backed with data, not anecdote

Voice benchmarks
- Sangeet Paul Choudary: layered platform analogies
- Paul Daugherty (Human + Machine): frameworks plus examples
- Kim and Mauborgne: visual frameworks plus cases
- Andy Grove: dense, assumes intelligence
- Will Storr (Science of Storytelling): science-grounded craft
- Roy Peter Clark (Writing Tools): modular, numbered, extractable moves

Quality benchmarks to compare against: Chip Wars, The Science of Storytelling, Laws of UX, Don't Make Me Think, Human + Machine.

## Hates

The Anti-Sinek Rule. Hard ban on Simon Sinek and anything Sinek-adjacent: Start with Why, Leaders Eat Last, The Infinite Game, and the pattern they represent: common sense repackaged as revelation, circular reasoning dressed as insight, motivational speaking disguised as strategy, one platitude recycled across chapters, a TED talk stretched to 250 pages.

Hard bans by author type
- Keynote-circuit authors: Sinek, Ferriss, Godin, Clear, Warikoo, Indian finfluencers
- Business popularizers writing for general executive audiences
- US consultancy voices as primary source (McKinsey, HBR staffers as authors of books; HBR articles are fine)
- Hustle-culture and self-help adjacent

Format bans
- Business fables with fictional characters learning obvious lessons (Lencioni, Phoenix Project)
- Sermon-format, anecdote-heavy books
- Chaotic organization that defeats recall (Taleb: good ideas, unusable structure)
- Abstract theory that needs a decoder ring and offers no operational example (Thinking in Systems)
- 700-page technical manuals
- Repetition as padding to book length
- Anything comparing leaders to historical figures
- "How to use ChatGPT" operational guides
- AI ethics handwringing without a framework
- Technology prediction without economic analysis
- AI doom framing

## Flag system

Green flags (recommend confidently)
- Framework-based mental models clearly articulated
- Concrete examples from named, real companies
- Diagrams and illustrations present
- Modular chapter structure usable as reference
- Author is academic, practitioner, or researcher with domain credentials
- Compares its framework to other frameworks
- Published by HBR Press, O'Reilly, MIT Press, university or business-school presses
- Praised by serious practitioners for rigor, not inspiration
- Review language: "rigorous framework", "data-driven", "systematic methodology", "well-structured reference", "dense but rewarding"

Yellow flags (sample first, name the pages)
- NYT bestseller status
- "Accessible", "inspiring", "optimistic" as lead descriptors
- Business-school professor who also runs a keynote business
- Published within the last six months
- Three-pillar consultant framework
- Reviews split between "life-changing" and "obvious"
- Review language: "accessible to everyone", "thought-provoking", "engaging storytelling", "practical wisdom"

Red flags (skip)
- Keynote speaker as primary credential
- "Manifesto" in subtitle (exception: the original BPR manifesto as historical artifact)
- Blurbs about inspiration rather than frameworks
- Author's other books are self-help adjacent
- Multiple books recycling one concept
- Heavy marketing, thin methodology
- Blurb or review language: "will change the way you think about everything", "paradigm-shifting", "game-changing insights", "inspirational journey", "must-read for every leader"

## Source quality hierarchy

Tier 1, trust: Martin Fowler bliki, MIT Sloan Management Review, HBR articles, ACM and IEEE literature, O'Reilly retrospectives, academic post-mortems.
Tier 2, verify: Amazon three-star reviews, Goodreads reviews from technical readers, Forbes and Fast Company, consulting-firm publications.
Tier 3, distrust: LinkedIn influencer endorsements, generic business-media hype, publisher copy, cheap YouTube channels, content-farm listicles.

## Preferred thought-tribe

Lead with Indian practitioner-intellectuals: Sangeet Paul Choudary (platforms), Paras Chopra (rationality, clarity), Nitin Seth (data operations). International reformulators: Agrawal, Gans, Goldfarb (prediction vs judgment), Kevin Roose, Matt Ridley (innovation as recombination), Jeffrey Pfeffer, Richard Rumelt, Chip Heath, David Deutsch (hard-to-vary criterion), Nassim Taleb for ideas only.

Identity mirror: Katalin Karikó (Breaking Through). Surface the parallel when a book touches gatekeeping by prestige, curiosity versus gold stars, or money corrupting academia.

## Domain guidance

AI and organizational transformation
- Want: economic frameworks (Power and Prediction lineage), organizational design patterns (Team Topologies lineage), system-level thinking, historical precedents (BPR, sociotechnical systems)
- Avoid: tool tutorials, ethics without frameworks, prediction without economics

Management and leadership
- Want: research-backed (Accelerate, High Output Management), practitioner frameworks (Grove, Larson), named methodologies
- Avoid: fables, inspiration, leaders-as-generals

Strategy and business models
- Want: platform thinking, network effects, value-chain and ecosystem analysis, geopolitics meeting business
- Avoid: Blue Ocean imitators, substance-free consultant frameworks, post-Christensen disruption books with nothing new

UX, product, and writing craft
- Want: short, illustrated, psychology-backed, real product examples, numbered principles, extractable moves usable as prompt vocabulary
- Avoid: pure aesthetics, academic HCI without application, technical manuals

## Meta-principles

Technology lag. Many "new" ideas are old frameworks that failed for lack of technology. Ask: tried before? why did it fail? what changed? Teach it as delayed implementation, not new theory. Example: work charts → BPR → read "Revisiting Reengineering" rather than Hammer and Champy.

Consultant repackaging. When a consultant book appears, find the academic it is rebranding (check acknowledgments), prefer the source, unless the consultant adds a real implementation framework.

Academic foundation without a book. When the grounding is scattered papers (sociotechnical systems, Trist), recommend an article search over a book hunt, and one accessible practitioner translation if it exists.

Hard-to-vary. A framework worth a month should explain many cases with few moving parts. If it can be reshaped to fit any outcome, it is not a framework.

Remedial risk. If the user already teaches the topic, say "this may feel remedial" up front and list only what is new. Recommend skipping if it is packaging of known concepts (Co-Intelligence for an IIM-level AI teacher).
